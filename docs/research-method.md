# Research method

This is a working protocol for public-source research, not a claim that every topic uses the same methodology.

## 1. Frame a bounded question

Define the unit of analysis, geography, period, key terms, and intended output. Identify whether the work is descriptive, comparative, explanatory, or exploratory.

Distinguish a research interest from a testable question and a hypothesis from a finding.

## 2. Select sources transparently

Record databases or repositories searched, exact queries, search dates, filters, inclusion rules, and exclusions. Prefer original records for claims about what an institution said or did.

Secondary sources can provide context and criticism. Evaluate them on their own merits and trace their evidence dependencies.

A source being official does not make every claim independently verified.

## 3. Maintain a source register

Use the [source-register template](../templates/source-register.csv). Record the publication date separately from the event date and access date. Record versions and stable identifiers when available.

Do not populate a bibliography with unread sources presented as reviewed evidence.

## 4. Separate evidence and interpretation

For each material claim, identify:
- What the source directly supports.
- Whether it is an observation, reported assertion, inference, or opinion.
- Which alternative explanations remain.
- Whether apparent corroboration depends on the same original source.
- What uncertainty limits the conclusion.

Use short excerpts only when necessary and within permission; otherwise paraphrase with attribution.

## 5. Analyze reproducibly

Define coding categories before full extraction. Pilot on a small subset, document revisions, and reapply changed definitions consistently.

Report the actual review process. Do not claim multiple reviewers or agreement statistics if only one person performed the work.

Keep analytical choices and exclusions visible. Record missing information as missing, not as evidence of failure.

## 6. Write with calibrated conclusions

Match the conclusion to the sample and method. Descriptive counts do not establish causation or broad prevalence. Confidence labels need an explanation, not just a score.

Include limitations, competing interpretations, and what new evidence would change the conclusion.

## 7. Publish and correct

Use a version and status label, source references, method, findings if any, and limitations. Corrections should explain what changed and whether conclusions were affected.

Publish only material appropriate for public release. Follow the [publication standards](publication-standards.md).
